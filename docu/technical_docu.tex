\begin{abstract}
We numerically study a bath-assisted quantum cooling channel for finite transverse-field Ising models with $N=4$–$8$ spins. The channel is implemented by simulating the system–bath evolution with a two-level thermal ancilla, followed by ancilla reset, and is averaged over local Pauli coupling operators and bath frequencies as suggested in %following the work of 
\cite{ding2025}. Using exact diagonalization, we compute the channel fixed point and compare it with the exact Gibbs state. %For the parameter regimes investigated, increasing the coupling strength generally reduces the number of channel iterations required for convergence but increases the fixed-point error relative to the Gibbs state. Intermediate coupling strengths provide a favorable empirical compromise between these effects.

Quantum mechanics connects with the tangible physical world by predicting  numerical values of actual measured quantities, i.e. by providing expectation values $\langle \mathcal{O} \rangle$ for the observables of interest. These expectation values only make sense once the state of the system, either pure or mixed, is fixed. A well known result from statistical mechanics is, that for systems that are in thermal equilibrium, the states under consideration are precisely the Gibbs states $\rho_{\beta}$ in the case of finite temperature or the ground-state $\ket{\Psi_0}$ in the case of zero-temperature. 
%
In particular, the time-ordered (resp. retarded) Green's function of a many-body Hamiltonian $H$ at a given inverse temperature $\beta$ is the expectation value for the \emph{correlation} function
\begin{align*} - i\langle \mathcal{T} c_i(t) c_j^{\dagger}(t')\rangle_{\beta}&=
-i \mbox{Tr}\, \rho_{\beta}\, \mathcal{T} c_i(t) c_j^{\dagger}(t')  \quad {\rm resp.} \\- i\theta(t-t')\langle \{c_i(t) c_j^{\dagger}(t')\}\rangle_{\beta}&=-i \mbox{Tr}\, \rho_{\beta}\, \theta(t-t')\{c_i(t), c_j^{\dagger}(t')\}.
\end{align*}

Once the (site-dependent) Green's function $G_{i,j}^T(t-t')$ (resp. $G_{i,j}^R(t-t')$) is known, several important one-particle properties can be effectively computed, e.g.
\begin{description}
    \item[\qquad Spectral function:] $A_{ij}(\omega)=-\frac{1}{\pi} {\rm Im } G_{i,j}^R(\omega)$ 
    \item[\qquad Density of states:] $D(\omega) = -\frac{1}{\pi N}   \mbox{Tr}\, {\rm Im }  G_R(\omega)$
    \item[\qquad Particle density:] $n_i = - i G_{ii}^T(0^-)$
    \item[\qquad Self-energy:] $\Sigma(\omega) = G_0(\omega)^{-1}-G(\omega)^{-1}$.
\end{description}

In concluding macro- and mesoscopic properties of a system, it is therefore essential to have reasonably accurate approximations of theses thermal states resp. the ground state available. One way to obtain thermal states is not by using some truncation or approximation scheme, but 
to engineer a specific interaction with a bath, that effectively enforces thermalization at a given temperature. Such a system has the advantage of being easily implementable on a quantum computer or quantum simulator, and allows for running times that often are merely polynomial in the system's parameters. 
In this case, the total system's Hamiltonian has three contributions:
\begin{align}\label{eq:hamiltonian_parts}
    H_{\rm cooling} = H_0 + H_{\rm int} + H_{\rm bath} 
\end{align}
and a cooling protocol would typically look like
\begin{align}\label{eq:qu_channel}
\rho\otimes\rho_b \longrightarrow U(t)(\rho\otimes\rho_b)\, U^{\dagger}(t)=\Tilde{\rho}  \longrightarrow \Tr_{\mathcal H_{\rm bath}} \Tilde{\rho}  =: \Lambda[\rho]
\end{align}
which corresponds to the following steps:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item prepare both the systems that is to be cooled and the bath: the first in a state $\rho$ and the latter in a thermal state $\rho_b$;
    \item  let the full system evolve for some time $t>0$. Here $U(t) = e^{-i t H}$ for time-independent Hamiltonians resp. $U(t)=\mathcal{T} \exp\left(-i \int_{0}^{ t} dt' H_{\rm cooling} (t') \right)$ for the general case (%Also note, that we are working 
    in natural units where $\hbar = 1$);
    \item discard the bath. %-subsystem.
\end{enumerate}
Note, that the last step will in general produce a density matrix rather than a pure state even if the initial state $\rho$ has been pure. Thus, if we think of repeated applications of this scheme, none of the involved states needs to necessarily be pure. 

The restricted action of \cref{eq:qu_channel} on the state-space $\mathcal{H}_0$ is usually called a quantum channel ($\Lambda[\,\cdot\,]$) and exhibits far richer dynamics than a purely unitary evolution. 

In this project work we report on the implementation of the cooling scheme proposed in \cite{ding2025} which follows along the lines of \cref{eq:qu_channel} and validate it against a known system Hamiltonian, the transverse field Ising model (TFIM) Hamiltonian and small system sizes $N=4-8$, where exact diagonalization is still possible and the thermal target state 
\begin{align}\label{eq:gibbs_state}
\rho_{\beta}= \frac{e^{-\beta H_0}}{{\rm Tr}\, e^{-\beta H_0}}
\end{align}
can be %may
explicitly be calculated.  In this situation $H_0 = H_{\rm TFIM}^{(h,J)}$ is actually a $2^N \times 2^N$ matrix, where $(h,J)$ are the parameters of the model (see \cref{sec:TFIM}). The bath can be taken as generic two-level system at inverse temperature $\beta$, and its Hamiltonian $H_{\rm bath}$  is just a $2\times 2$ matrix. The interaction Hamiltonian $H_{\rm int}^{(\alpha, A, f, g)}$ is an off-diagonal $2^{N+1} \times 2^{N+1}$ block matrix that couples the two systems (again $\alpha, A, f, g$ are specific parameters of the coupling and will be explained later).


\section{Cooling as quantum operation}
The two-level system utilized as bath is fully described by its eigenenergies: here they are taken as $\pm\omega/2$, where $\omega>0$ is a frequency parameter. The fiducial state for the bath subsystem is the two-dimensional Gibbs-state $\rho_{\beta}\propto e^{\tfrac{\omega}{2}\beta Z }$.

The basic building blocks for the coupling terms are of the form
\begin{align}\label{eq:single_cooling_process}
    A_S \otimes \sigma_+ +A_S^{\dagger} \otimes \sigma_- ,
\end{align}
each of which describes the physical process of transferring energy from the system to the bath and vice versa. There are a few requirements, both technical and heuristic on how the operator $A_S$ is chosen. Most importantly, $A_S$ itself should be treated as parameter and is chosen from a pre-specified set of operators $\mathcal{A}$. To de-clutter the algorithms, $\mathcal{A}$ is usually chosen to be invariant under Hermitian conjugation and sign inversion. Furthermore $\|A\|\le 1$, in particular the spectra should be universally bounded. It is essential that $\mathcal{A}$ contains operators that are not diagonal in the energy-eigenbasis but connects states from different energies, making cooling possible in the first place. In the case that $H_0$ exhibits different symmetry sectors, it is essential that $\mathcal{A}$ mixes those sectors, e.g. if we have a translation-invariant chain of spins, the operators from $\mathcal{A}$ should address the sites individually.  

The term in \cref{eq:single_cooling_process} is multiplied with $\alpha f(t)$, where $\alpha >0$ is fixed and $f(t)$ a time-dependent coupling strength. We require $\int dt \, |f(t)|^2  = 1$, so $f(t)$ resp. its Fourier-transform $\hat{f}(\omega)$ should be thought of as frequency filter and is usually taken as square-root of a Gaussian $\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)$. $\hat{f}(\omega)$ suppresses transitions from the system to the bath and vice versa that correspond to an allowed detuning of Bohr-frequencies from the bath frequency on a scale set by $1/\sigma$. Thus $H_{\rm int}(t) =  H_{\rm int}^{(\alpha, A, \sigma)}(t)$. 

For the time evolution a duration $T>0$ is fixed and the fiducial states are evolved from $-T$ to $T$. Note, that the negative times are not essential and could easily be avoided by redefining $f$. However, it is beneficial to preserve the symmetry of $f$ via $f(-t)=f(t)$ over the time interval $[-T,T]$: In this case also the Fourier transform $\hat{f}(\omega)$ is again real (and even) and thus allows for an easy interpretation as frequency filter-function. The full Hamiltonian %, however, 
is now time dependent which makes the computation of the time-propagation more intricate:
\begin{align}\label{eq:timedependent_H}
U(t_0 \rightarrow t_1) = \mathcal{T}\exp\left(-i \int_{t_0}^{ t_1} d\tau \left(H_0 +  \alpha f(\tau)( A_S \otimes \sigma_+ +A_S^{\dagger} \otimes \sigma_- )+
H_{\rm bath}\right) \right).
\end{align}


While an analog simulation would produce the correct time evolution intrinsically, on a digital quantum simulator we have to program the time evolution explicitly and do so with second order Trotterization for some time $\Delta t$
\begin{align}\label{eq:trotterization}\notag
U(t\rightarrow t+\Delta t)&\approx \exp\left(-i\int_{t+\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}}^{t+\Delta t}d\tau H_{\rm int}(\tau)\right)e^{-i(H_0+H_{\rm bath})\Delta t} \exp\left(-i\int_{t}^{t+\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}}d\tau H_{\rm int}(\tau)\right)\\
\end{align}
and then approximate the integrals by using the value at the midpoint as constant approximation in order to preserve symmetry: \SA{maybe explain importance}
\begin{align}\label{eq:trotterization_with_freezing}
U(t\rightarrow t+\Delta t)&\approx e^{-i H_{\rm int}(t+\tfrac{\Delta t}{2})\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}}e^{-i(H_0+H_{\rm bath})\Delta t} e^{-i H_{\rm int}(t+\tfrac{\Delta t}{2})\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}}.
\end{align}
%\SA{($\frac{1}{2}\Delta t \to \Delta t /2 $?)}
In the cooling algorithm itself, the interval $[-T,T]$ is divided into $M$ subintervals of length $\tau$ and each subinterval is evolved according to \cref{eq:trotterization_with_freezing}. Note, that freezing $H_{\rm int}$ at the subinterval-midpoint is just a convenient numerical choice, but not necessarily optimal. Using different evaluation points for the quadrature of $f$, or even exact evaluation of the $f$-integration, would not necessarily improve the order of the approximation, but potentially reduce the constant of the error term. However, we will not pursue this particular direction further.

\section{Transverse field Ising model}\label{sec:TFIM}
The benchmark system which is used for validation is given by the Hamiltonian
\begin{align}\label{eq:TFIM_Hamiltonian}
    H(J,h) = -J\sum_{<i,j>} \sigma^{(z)}_{i}\sigma^{(z)}_{j} - h\sum_{i=1}^{N} \sigma^{(x)}_i 
\end{align} 
and models a 1D chain of spins in $z$-direction in a homogeneous magnetic field $h$ that is aligned transversely with respect to the orientation of the spin-spin interaction. For simplicity we only consider periodic boundary conditions (i.e. the pair $<1, N>$ is included in the sum) and an even number of sites.

Due to the next-neighbor interaction there exists a global $\mathbb{Z}_2$-symmetry. Flipping each site commutes with the Hamiltonian: for $P=\sigma^{(x)}_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot \sigma^{(x)}_{N}$ we have $P^2=I$ and $P H(J,h) P^{\dagger}=H(J,h)$, while for $Q=\sigma^{(z)}_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot \sigma^{(z)}_{N}$ we have $Q H(J,h) Q^{\dagger}=H(J,-h)$.

The relevant parameter of this system is $J/h$, the ratio of the energy scale of the spin-spin interaction to the potential energy of the spins in the magnetic field. To keep the energy scales of $H(J,h)$ comparable while sweeping $J$ and $h$, we usually will impose the normalization
\begin{align}\label{eq:JHnormalization}
    J \to J/\sqrt{J^2+h^2}\notag \\
    h \to h/\sqrt{J^2+h^2}
\end{align} which leaves the underlying physics unchanged and puts energies, gaps and dynamical timescales essentially on the same scale.  For positive $J,h$ with $J\ll h$ a configuration with all spins aligned in $\ket{\uparrow}_x$ is favored (paramagnetic phase) and the spectrum is gaped with $\Delta \approx 2|h|$. 

For $J\gg h$ the ground state in the thermodynamical limit $N\to\infty$ is twofold degenerate: for $J>0$ all spins are either aligned in $\ket{\uparrow}_z$ direction or in $\ket{\downarrow}_z$ direction (ferromagnetic phase) resp. aligned alternately for $J<0$. The gap to the bulk is $\Delta_{\rm bulk}\approx 2 |J-h|$. In the finite case with non-vanishing field $h$, the degeneracy is lifted and the even parity combination of the above mentioned ferromagnetically aligned states is the ground state, whereas the odd parity combination is the first excited state. The splitting between these two state, i.e. the gap to the first excited stays exponentially small in $N$. The gap to the bulk remains finite even in the thermodynamical limit as long as the parameter $J/h$ stays away from the quantum critical region $J\approx h$. Here the gap closes asymptotically for $N\to \infty$ with $\Delta_{\rm bulk} \approx \frac1N$. 
In our numerical analysis $(N=4-8)$ the gap $\Delta_N:= E_1-E_0$ is always finite except in the trivial case $h=0$. For a detailed finite-size analysis of this system we refer to \cite{oshikawa2019}.


The spectrum of $H(J,h)$ can be computed exactly by mapping the TFIM to a free fermionic system. First one introduces the Jordan-Wigner transformation which relates spin-strings to fermionic operators:
\begin{align}\label{eq:JW-trafo}
\hat{c}_j = \left(\prod_{l<j} \sigma^{(x)}_{l} \right) \frac{\sigma^{(z)}_{j}-i\sigma^{(y)}_{j}}{2}, \quad \hat{c}_j^{\dagger} = \left(\prod_{l<j} \sigma^{(x)}_{l} \right) \frac{\sigma^{(z)}_{j}+i\sigma^{(y)}_{j}}{2}
\end{align}
This transforms \cref{eq:TFIM_Hamiltonian} to a quadratic fermionic Hamiltonian which then can be diagonalized via momentum basis and a suitable Bogoliubov transformation. The end result is a free fermionic Hamiltonian which is diagonal in the number basis $\hat{n}_k= \hat{\gamma}_k^{\dagger} \hat{\gamma}_k$ of the corresponding Bogoliubov quasiparticles:
\begin{align}\label{eq:TFIM_ff}
    H = \sum_k \varepsilon_k (J,h)\left( \hat{n}_k - \frac12\right)
\end{align}   
with dispersion relation
\begin{align}\label{eq:TFIM_dispersion}
   \varepsilon_k(J,h) = 2\sqrt{(h-J\cos k)^2 + J^2\sin^2 k} \quad {\rm for} \quad k = \pm \frac{\pi}{N}, \pm \frac{2\pi}{N}, \ldots
\end{align}
In particular for $J=h$ one gets
\begin{align}\label{eq:H_critical}
    H = \sum_k 4J | \sin \tfrac{k}{2}| \left( \hat{n}_k - \tfrac12\right).
\end{align}

The equivalence of TFIM to a free fermionic Hamiltonian allows for a priori estimates on the integer mixing time of the cooling algorithm, that would be much harder to come by for other models, see Appendix F.2 in \cite{ding2025}. Therein an $\mathcal{O}(N^2\log \tfrac{N}{\varepsilon})$ dependence on system size and tolerated error is asserted. However this approach hinges on the choice $\mathcal{A}=\{\pm \gamma_k, \pm \gamma_k^{\dagger}\}$ of coupling operators, which are \emph{not} simply Pauli operators on individual sites but rather nonlocal combinations thereof. In our implementation we aim to have both operations $P$ and $Q$ available and thus are more interested in sets of coupling operators that contain $\sigma^{(x)}_{1}, \dots, \sigma^{(x)}_{N}$ \emph{and} $\sigma^{(z)}_{1}, \dots, \sigma^{(z)}_{N}$

\section{Parameter dependence}
Let us quickly review the parameters we encountered so far:

\begin{description}
    \item[coupling strength $\alpha$] determines the magnitude of the interaction part in the cooling Hamiltonian, i.e. the maximal rate at which cooling processes mediated by the bath can take place. To allow for the description of the cooling process via perturbative Lindblad dynamics $\alpha$ is treated as small parameter in \cite{ding2025}. However, larger coupling allows for higher energy transfer from the system to the bath and therefore increases cooling. In \cite{wang2025} the validity of the cooling scheme is established also for $\alpha = O(1)$. The numerical experiments of this work underscore these findings.  
    
    \item[temporal width $\sigma$] denotes standard deviation of a Gaussian filter function in time space. $\sigma$ tunes scale at which transitions from bath to system and vice versa with larger than $\frac{1}{\sigma}$ detuning are suppressed. A safe choice is 
    $\frac{\hbar}{\sigma}\sim \|H_0\|$ resp. the energy bandwidth of the system that is to be cooled.

    \item[frequency cutoff $\omega_{\max}$] determines the range of transition frequencies that are sampled. In \cite{ding2025} this cutoff is necessary since the transition frequencies are sampled uniformly
    \begin{align}\label{eq:omega_distribution}
        g(\omega)=\frac{1}{\omega_{\max}}\mathbb{1}_{[0,\omega_{\max}]}(\omega).
    \end{align}
    The cutoff has to be large enough to include the relevant Bohr-frequencies of the system. A safe choice is the energy bandwidth $\omega_{\max}\geq  2 \|H\|\geq E_{\max}-E_{\min}$. In practice however it is not necessary to include \emph{all} but sufficiently many frequencies. Therefore a cutoff at the median transition frequency serves as a good starting point. It should be noted that a uniform distribution is merely convenient, but not necessarily the optimal choice.  In light of \cref{fig:bohr_bandwiths,fig:bohr_quantiles} $\sigma=8$ is safe for TFIM $N=4$, but $\sigma \approx 5$ is still sensible for $N=4-8$.

    \item[interaction time $T$] determines the finite time interval $[-T,T]$ during which system and bath interact. During the interaction the coupling constant is modulated with the Gaussian $f$. Increasing $T$ decreases the error due to ignoring $f$ outside $[-T,T]$ but increases the simulation time necessary to evolve the combined Hamiltonian $H_{\rm cooling}$ which itself is subject to discretization a Trotterization errors. In \cite{ding2025} it is assumed that $T\gg \sigma$ in order to give convergence guarantees.

    \item[Trotter step size $\tau$ and order] determine the discretization scheme of the time evolution on the interval $[-T,T]$. There are
    \begin{align*}
        M=\left\lceil\frac{2T}{\tau}\right\rceil.
    \end{align*}
    Trotter steps necessary. The chosen scheme is usually up to second order and thus avoids negative weights in the approximation
    Decreasing $\tau$ reduces the Trotter error but increases the circuit depth and number of numerical evaluations. Consequently, $\tau$ has to resolve both the system dynamics generated by $H_0$ and the system-bath interaction. As heuristic $\tau$ should be small compared to the times scale set by the free evolution of the system Hamiltonian and the interaction with the bath. 

    \item[frequency spacing $\Delta\omega$] resp. quadrature rule for the integration of the bath frequency sampling density $g(\omega)$. In the numerical implementation of the algorithm $[0, \omega_{\rm max}]$ is replaced by a discrete frequency grid. The spacing has to be sufficiently small to resolve the relevant Bohr-frequencies. A too coarse frequency grid can miss relevant transitions and can therefore change both the cooling rate and the averaged channel.

    \item[coupling operators $\mathcal{A}$] determine which transitions between energy eigenstates can be induced by interactions with the bath. In each application of the cooling channel, an operator $A_S$ is sampled from $\mathcal{A}$. The set has to connect relevant parts of the spectrum. Otherwise conserved quantities or disconnected invariant subspaces can prevent convergence to a unique thermal state. For spin systems, a natural choice is a set of local Pauli operators, provided not all of them commute with the system Hamiltonian.

\end{description}

\section{Lindbladian approximation}
The main approximation result in \cite{ding2025} is that the cooling channel $\lambda$ can approximated by unitary time evolution of the system plus a Lindbladian action:
\begin{align}\label{eq:channelapprox}
\Lambda[\rho] \approx \Lambda_{\mathcal{M}}[\rho]=\mathcal{U}(T) \circ \exp{\left( \mathcal{M}\alpha^2 \right)} \circ \mathcal{U}(T)     
\end{align}
where 
\begin{align}
\mathcal{U}(T)[\rho] = \exp(-iH_0T)\rho \exp(iH_0T)
\end{align}
and $\mathcal{M}$ is the generator of a carefully constructed Lindbladian that preserves the Gibbs state $\rho_{\beta}$. It is shown that this approximation is arbitrarily close to an Lindbladian channel that has error $O(\frac{\alpha^4 T^4}{\sigma^2})$. In particular, we see that the Lindbladian part of the evolution evolves with an effective time increment of $\alpha^2$. Since 
\begin{align}
\mathcal{U}(T)[\rho_{\beta}] = \frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}}\exp(-iH_0T)\exp(-\beta H_0) \exp(iH_0T) = \frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}}\exp(-\beta H_0) = \rho_{\beta}
\end{align} leaves Gibbs states invariant and the Lindbladian generator fulfills $\mathcal{M}[\rho_{\beta}]=0$ by construction, the RHS of \cref{eq:channelapprox} has $\rho_{\beta}$ as a fixed point. Hence, as long as the approximation is valid, $\rho_{\beta}$ is an approximate fixed point of the cooling channel $\Lambda[\cdot]$.  In \cite{ding2025} it 
is proven that under certain conditions a matrix norm, namely $\| X\| = \|\rho_{\beta}^{-1/4} X \rho]\rho_{\beta}^{-1/4}\|_2$, can be chosen such that $\Lambda_{\mathcal{M}}$ is a contraction with respect to this norm, hence establishing uniqueness of the fixed point. However, uniqueness of the exact channel $\Lambda$ does not necessarily follow. Also it has to be noted that these arguments are perturbative and hence require $\alpha \ll 1$. 


\section{Implementation as quantum circuit}
The \textsc{pennylane} python package was used to implement concrete realizations of the quantum channel. Here 'concrete' means, that the total cooling Hamiltonian $H$ is built using a specific jump-operator $A\in \mathcal{A}$ and a specific Bohr frequency $\omega\in [0, \omega_0]$ and evolved from time $t=-T$ to time $t=T$. The Trotterization from eq (4) is directly implemented into the circuit, meaning once the total evolution time $2T$ and the sub-step time $\tau$ is chosen, the quantum circuit is built according to this specifications. It consists of $M+1$ layers, each effecting a time increment of $\tau$. In \cref{fig:qc} a circuit which implements two cooling steps each of which is uses individual Trotter steps of length $\tau = T/2$. Here $W(\tau)$ denotes the time-evolution of $H_{\rm int}$, whereas the time evolution of $H_{\rm bath}$ is implemented directly as $Z$-rotation and the time evolution of $H_0$ is obtained by Trotterization with the same step size $\tau$.

After each cooling step, the auxiliary qubit is measured and prepared in the same Gibbs-state of the two-level system. The \textsc{pennylane} framework offers two approaches on how to implement this reset:

\begin{itemize}
    \item we choose the initial state of the auxiliary qubit probabilistically according to the statistics of the Gibbs state, i.e. 
    \begin{align}
        p_0=p(\ket{0}) = \frac{e^{\beta \tfrac{\omega}{2}}}{2\cosh{\beta \tfrac{\omega}{2}}} \quad\mbox{resp.}\quad p_1=p(\ket{1}) = \frac{e^{-\beta \tfrac{\omega}{2}}}{2\cosh{\beta \tfrac{\omega}{2}}}.
    \end{align}
    \item we run the simulation in mixed mode, meaning states are not assumed to be pure but mixed instead. In this case we can implement the constant 2-dimensional quantum channel 
    \begin{align}\label{eq:constant_channel}
        \rho \mapsto \sum_{i,j}  \underbrace{\sqrt{p_i}\ket{i}\bra{j}}_{K_{ij}}\,\rho\, \underbrace{\sqrt{p_i}\ket{j}\bra{i}}_{K_{ij}^{\dagger}} = \sum_{i} p_i \ket{i}\bra{i}\, \rm{Tr}\rho = \rho_{\left(\beta \tfrac{\omega}{2}\right)},
    \end{align} where we have used the channel representation with Kraus-operators, which can be implemented directly in \textsc{pennylane}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Simulation using superoperator representation}
A quantum channel is a map that maps density matrices to density matrices, i.e. $\Lambda: \mathbb{C}^{2^N\times 2^N} \to \mathbb{C}^{2^N\times 2^N}$. If we view $\Lambda$ as a transformation of matrices, it is common to use the name superoperator instead. $\Lambda$ can then be treated as a $4^N\times 4^N$ matrix. The identification $\mathbb{C}^{2^N\times 2^N}\cong \mathbb{C}^{4^N}$ is called ,,vectorization''.

\subsection{Kraus representation}
Every quantum channel has also - at least one - Kraus representation, i.e. can be written in the form
\begin{align*}
    \Lambda[\rho] = \sum_i K_i \rho K_i^{\dagger}.
\end{align*}

Since the system's state space $\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_0\otimes \mathcal{H}_b$, where the bath's state space is just two-dimensional, this allows for some simplification: Let us denote by $U=U(t_m,t_{m+1}$ the time evolution of composite system including bath for a particular time interval. We can write
\begin{align*}
U =
\begin{pmatrix}
U_{00} & U_{10} \\
U_{01} & U_{11},
\end{pmatrix}
\end{align*}
where $U_{ij} = \bra{i}_{b} U  \ket{j}_{b}$ is an operator $\mathcal{H}_0 \to \mathcal{H}_0$, i.e. a $2^N \times 2^N$ matrix. Applying $U\rho U^{\dagger}$ to a density matrix of the form $\rho_0 \otimes \sum_{i} p_i  \ket{i}_{b}\bra{i}_{b}$ we get
\begin{align*}
     \Tr_{\mathcal H_{\rm bath}} U\rho U^{\dagger} = \sum_{ij} p_j  U_{ij}\rho_0 U_{ij}^{\dagger}.
\end{align*} This involves only matrix-products of $2^N \times 2^N$ matrices, which is advantageous to multiplying $2^{N+1} \times 2^{N+1}$ matrices.

\subsection{Averaged channel}
The cooling algorithm proposed by Ding et.al. is probabilistic in the sense, that end-to-end guarantees apply to the \emph{average} channel, where an expectation value is taken both with respect to the jump operators and the Bohr frequencies:
\begin{align}\label{eq:avg_channel}
    \overline{\Lambda}\,[\rho]:= \mathbb{E}_{A, \omega} \Lambda(A,\omega)\,[\rho]. 
\end{align}

In choosing the underlying probability distributions on $\mathcal{A}$ resp. $[0,\omega_{\max}$ we follow Ding et. al. and choose uniform distributions on both sets. At least for $\mathcal{A}$ this seems to be the canonical choice - at least if the jump operators correspond to physical sites and the Hamiltonian is translation invariant. 

In this work we focus on the set $\mathcal{A}=\{\pm \sigma_i^{(x)}, \pm \sigma_i^{(z)}: i = 1,\ldots N\}$ and the uniform probability assigning each operator $\frac{1}{4N}$.
As simple choice for the set of Bohr frequencies $[0,\omega_{\rm}]$ we choose uniform distribution as well. Here however there is a priori no need to exclude other distributions and it could turn out that e.g. distributions 
that are skewed towards higher frequencies have an advantage. In this work however we don't pursue this particular degree of freedom.

To realize the averaged channel $\overline{\Lambda}$ numerically we approximate
\begin{align}\label{eq:avg_channel_quadrature}
    \int_0^{\omega_{\rm max}} d\omega\, \Lambda(A,\omega) = \frac{1}{k\omega_{\rm max}}\sum_{i=1}^k \Lambda(A,\omega_i) 
\end{align} by some quadrature formula, e.g. by the midpoint-formula where $\omega_i = \frac{2i+1}{k}\omega_{\rm max}$. The average channel is thus approximated by the object
\begin{align}\label{eq:avg_channel_approx}
\frac{1}{4N  k \omega_{\rm max} }\sum_{A\in\mathcal A} \sum_{i=1}^k \Lambda(A, \tfrac{2i+1}{k}\omega_{\rm max})
\end{align}


up to order $O(\tfrac{1}{k^2})$. Thus the number of individual contributions for a Hamiltonian on $N$ sites scales with $4N\cdot k$. To keep the computational demand in check we let $k = 10$, so for computing the average channel for $N=4,6,8$ we have to run trajectories of length $160, 240$ and $320$ each. 
%
%  \item[number of channel iterations $n$] determines how long the cooling channel is applied. It does not affect the fixed point of the exact channel but determines how closely the iterated state approaches it. In the weak-coupling regime, one application corresponds approximately to a Lindbladian evolution time $\alpha^2$, so that the number of iterations required for a fixed effective evolution time scales approximately as $\alpha^{-2}$.
%
%    \item[number of channel samples $N_{\mathrm{samp}}$] determines how accurately the averaged channel is approximated when the average over bath frequencies and coupling operators is evaluated by sampling. Increasing $N_{\mathrm{samp}}$ reduces statistical fluctuations but increases the numerical resp. experimental cost. This parameter is absent if the average is evaluated exactly.

\section{Numerical Experiments}
\subsection{h-sweep}
As first numerical experiment we study how the cooling channel behaves as the transverse field of the TFIM is varied. We sweep the ratio $h/J$ from the ferromagnetic regime $h/J<1$, through the critical region around $h/J=1$, into the paramagnetic regime $h/J>1$. To avoid changing the overall energy scale of the system simultaneously, $J$ and $h$ are normalized according to \cref{eq:JHnormalization}. For each $h/J$-value of the sweep the averaged cooling channel $\overline{\Lambda}$ is constructed explicitly according to \cref{eq:avg_channel}. Its fixed point $\rho^*$ is obtained from the eigenvector corresponding to the eigenvalue $\lambda=1$. We compare four different coupling strengths $\alpha=0.25,0.5,0.75,1.0$, while all remaining parameters of the cooling channel are kept fixed:
$T=25$ with $\tau = 0.1$ (i.e. $250$ sub-steps per Trotterization), $\sigma = 2$ ensuring that the temporal window $[-T,T]$ is large compared to $\frac{1}{\sigma}$. The frequency cutoff is chosen near the systems bandwidth $\omega_{\max}=8$ and inverse temperature is set to $\beta=1$.

Convergence is measured in number of iterations starting from the pure state $\rho_0=\ket{0000}\bra{0000}$ till the trace distance
\begin{align}\label{eq:tracedistance}
    D(\overline{\Lambda}^n[\rho_0],\rho_*) = \tfrac 12 \left\| \overline{\Lambda}^n[\rho_0] - \rho_*\right\|_1
\end{align} is less than $\epsilon = 0.05$. We note that this notion of convergence differs from the one used in \cite{ding2025} insofar as no supremum over initial states is taken. Instead, as is common in quantum computation, the initial state $\ket{0000}$ is fixed.

In particular there seems to be nothing special about the critical region $h/J\approx 1$. We also see quite different qualitative behavior for the four tested coupling strengths $\alpha = 0.25, 0.5., 0.75$ and $1.0$. While in general the number of necessary iterations decreases for larger $\alpha$, the functional relationship changes significantly. The smallest $\alpha=0.25$ exhibits an almost exponential decay with respect to $h/J$ (linear in the semi-logarithmic plot \cref{fig:iterations_logplot}) where as $\alpha = 0.75$ and $\alpha = 1.0$ display a nonlinear approach to a constant baseline. $\alpha=0.5$ is qualitatively closer to the linear case, but seems to have slightly different slopes in the para- resp. ferromagnetic region, see \cref{fig:iterations_fitted}.
The number of iterations till fixed-point closeness does not decrease uniformly across the parameter space. In the non ferromagnetic regimen higher coupling means faster convergence, although there is only marginal improvement from $\alpha = 0.75$ out on. Qualitatively a transition from log-linear to non-linear takes place between $0.5\le \alpha \le 0.75$.

To study the spectral properties of the channel with respect to its fixed point behavior we introduce the quantity
\begin{align}\label{eq:spectral_separation}
    \Delta_{\mathrm{sep}} = \min_{\lambda\neq 1}|1-\lambda|.
\end{align} Note that \cref{eq:spectral_separation} is in general different from the spectral \emph{gap}
\begin{align}\label{eq:spectral_gap}
    \Delta_{\mathrm{gap}} = 1- \max_{\lambda\neq 1}|\lambda|\le \Delta_{\mathrm{sep}},
\end{align}
only for real nonnegative subleading eigenvalue the two notion coincide. Since, by perturbative arguments we know that - at least in the weak coupling regime - the channel is guaranteed to have am almost thermal fixed point, we are mostly interested in the local behavior around this fixed point rather than in the global gap. However, numerical inspection of explicit spectra indicate $\lambda_2$ being positive and real, so that $\Delta_{\mathrm{sep}} \approx \Delta_{\mathrm{gap}}$ is a sensible working hypothesis for our experimental runs, cf. \cref{fig:spectrum_example}.
